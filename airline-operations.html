Business problem

Arrival punctuality and cancellation rates can be calculated from public data, but airline operations questions get harder when they involve aircraft history. A missing flight, misread time zone or changed airport identifier can make a supposed turnaround wrong.

I wanted to answer two practical questions: How dependable are basic operational KPIs? And can consecutive reported flights from the same aircraft be linked without inventing activity we cannot observe?

Data and reporting population

I used the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance files for January and July 2025, filtering the reporting-airline code to DL. This is a sample of reported U.S. domestic operations, not Delta's complete global network or necessarily every Delta-marketed partner flight.

Source ZIP hashes, source-field contracts, exclusions and metric denominators are documented in the data-quality results. January and July are separate descriptive samples, not a controlled seasonal experiment.

Where would I investigate first?

I segmented the same verified DL reporting-carrier domestic flights into those departing Atlanta (ATL) and those departing every other airport. The source rows and arrival denominators reconcile exactly with the overall January and July KPIs above. This is an exploratory comparison, not an estimate of an ATL airport effect.

ATL-origin flights had lower observed arrival punctuality in both months. However, the route mix, destination, scheduled departure bank, aircraft allocation and operating conditions differ. This comparison alone cannot tell us whether a process at ATL caused the gap.

Reported positive late-aircraft counts: Jan 1,172 / 3,821 ATL and 4,454 / 10,481 other; Jul 2,419 / 6,499 ATL and 8,184 / 16,419 other. All 37,220 late-arrival records across both months had complete cause details, and their reported cause minutes reconciled to arrival delay. BTS causes are carrier-reported attribution on flights arriving at least 15 minutes late, not an independently linked causal explanation of the prior flight. Multiple delay causes may appear on the same flight.

Operational next question: Within ATL-origin departures, which scheduled banks, destinations and routes explain the largest share of late arrivals after accounting for the flight mix? I would investigate those groups before recommending changes to turnaround, staffing or dispatch. Neither the raw gap nor the reported cause mix supports a specific intervention yet.

Can we observe aircraft connections?

Gate times were checked against airport time zones, scheduled and actual elapsed times, and reported delays. A historical airport-code change created a concrete data-quality issue: PBI was missing from a current airport mapping even though the 2025 flights still used that code. I added one documented historical-code time-zone exception and repeated all clock checks.

53,047January candidate same-tail, same-airport connections
71,881July candidate connections
77 / 74 minMedian observed candidate ground gap · Jan / Jul

Each candidate requires consecutive reported flights from one tail, matching airports, valid gate timestamps and a non-overlapping ground interval of 20–360 minutes. Those limits are analytical screening rules, not certified airline turnaround minimums. Unreported international operations or other missing legs can interrupt the real aircraft history.

What happens after a late inbound?

Within the selected candidate pairs, I compared the share of next departures delayed by at least 15 minutes after a late previous arrival versus a not-late previous arrival.

The overall association is substantial, but it does not prove that the inbound delay caused the next departure delay. A useful warning came from July's longer 120–360-minute gap group: the pattern reversed, with 28.90% next-departure delays after late inbounds versus 40.39% after not-late inbounds. Common congestion, scheduling, weather, missing flights and selecting on realized ground intervals can distort these comparisons.

What this means for an operations team

The two-month sample can support transparent historical punctuality reporting and screening of observed aircraft connections. It does not yet support full fleet utilization, maintenance events, an operationally complete rotation map, a causal delay-propagation estimate or a claim of preventable delay savings.

My recommended decision is to use the validated KPIs for descriptive operational monitoring and require a broader reporting window, completeness review and more rigorous controls before making stronger disruption-recovery claims.

Validation, exceptions and reproducibility

Independent verification: a second implementation reconstructed arrival UTC from actual elapsed minutes and reproduced the tail connections and late-departure count quadrants using SQLite window functions. It exactly matched the primary analysis for both months. The final run passed 27 offline tests.

Exceptions retained: three January flight records failed the scheduled-arrival clock check, and two July observed same-tail pairs overlapped. Neither was silently repaired. The same-tail connection results remain candidates, not verified complete aircraft rotations.
