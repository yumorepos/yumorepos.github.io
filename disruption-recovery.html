Operational question

When flights cannot all operate exactly as planned, how should an airline weigh cancellations against delayed operations under aircraft constraints? A recovery strategy must consider airport positioning, the time an aircraft becomes available, minimum ground time, flight duration and the fact that each flight can operate once—or be canceled.

I built a small educational simulator, not a dispatch system. Its purpose is to demonstrate model formulation, realistic constraint logic at the toy-scenario level, careful benchmark selection and a willingness to question whether an apparent improvement is credible.

Model and validation method

The model enumerates feasible paths for each fictional aircraft, then uses a binary mixed-integer set-partitioning formulation to choose a compatible combination of paths and cancellations. The objective adds illustrative cancellation penalties and passenger-delay penalties. The solver certifies an optimum only inside the finite flight set and discrete delay options that were enumerated.

01 / FORMULATEConstrain aircraft pathsAirport continuity, availability, turnaround, delay choices and no flight reuse.
02 / VALIDATEChallenge the solverIndependent small permutation oracle checks an overtaking counterexample.
03 / BENCHMARKCompare feasible policiesCost-aware greedy, fly-first greedy, and all-cancel on the same seeded cases.

30 reproducible synthetic scenarios

The benchmark generated 30 fictional five-flight, two-tail problems with an explicit seed and scenario checksums. Every method used the same flight options and objective units. The cost-aware greedy comparator, unlike the original fly-first policy, considers canceling a flight when its immediate delay penalty exceeds the cancellation penalty.

Against cost-aware greedy, the MILP was better in 12 cases, tied in 18 and worse in none. The mean difference was 90.05 illustrative units. The much larger difference against fly-first is less meaningful because that policy is intentionally weak. These results are not real airline savings.

The operational takeaway

A stronger baseline substantially changes how impressive an optimization result appears. The useful lesson is not a monetary percentage: it is that aircraft positioning, operating constraints and an explicit cancellation option can be formulated, tested and compared against transparent decision policies.

That is the analytical skill I would bring to an operations team: define the decision, explain the constraints, compare credible alternatives and state clearly what the evidence does—and does not—support.

What remains outside this model

The experiment does not include real airline schedules, crew legality, maintenance, airport slots, actual passenger connections, aircraft type compatibility, curfews, recovery across days or a complete network model. It is exact only within enumerated paths and discrete delays and stops if enumeration becomes too large. The scenarios are intentionally tiny and highly structured, so their results cannot establish industry-scale optimizer performance or practical recovery savings.

The full 30-case benchmark passed 11 focused tests and was run a second time with byte-for-byte identical output. The older ad-hoc synthetic benchmark used a different generator and is not directly comparable. No frozen Network Lab route-screening finding was changed.
