Why I built it

I studied aviation management and have worked in travel operations, so I wanted to understand how an airline might evaluate routes it does not currently serve. I built Network Lab to explore that question using publicly available data, without pretending those sources offer the same detail as an airline's internal planning systems.

What I built

I created a Python and DuckDB workflow that brings together U.S. and Canadian aviation and economic data, including passenger demand, airline service, operating costs, and fuel prices. It records where demand is observed versus modeled, reconstructs competition, and applies documented assumptions to compare route economics.

For a fixed aircraft size and a predefined schedule menu, it produces LAUNCH, MONITOR, or PASS research-screening classifications. It does not optimize an airline's fleet, frequencies, or network.

The important part: correcting the model

The initial implementation had two material errors. It compared bidirectional passenger demand against departures counted in only one direction, and it missed connecting competitors by treating operating-airline certificates as customer-facing airline brands.

Those mistakes made some routes look more attractive than they should have. I corrected the logic, re-ran the screens, and kept the less favorable results rather than defending the original conclusions.

THE FINDING

More complexity did not establish better predictions.

In the historical WestJet Calgary replay, the full model showed no detectable incremental ranking value over a demand-only baseline for effects larger than the study could resolve. Smaller improvements remain unresolved.

The comparison covered only ten distinct launch events, limiting what can be concluded. A negative or inconclusive result is still valuable when it changes the scope of the claim.

What this demonstrates

The main achievement is not predicting which route will succeed. It is building traceable data workflows, checking units and airline mappings, comparing a complex approach with a simple baseline, and reducing claims when the evidence does not support them.

These are the habits I want to bring into aviation operations and analytics work: data quality, reproducibility, operational understanding, and clear communication about uncertainty.

Evidence & limitations

The August 17, 2026 independent release review reports 206 passing tests and 29 reproducible artifacts using the full local warehouse. Those are reported project-review results, not tests re-run as part of this website update. The full warehouse is not included in GitHub, and the code-only review cannot independently reproduce the data-dependent metrics.

Some transborder demand inputs are historical or modeled. Fare estimates, competition proxies, and the limited replay sample also constrain the results. This remains research-grade decision support, not commercially validated route forecasting.
